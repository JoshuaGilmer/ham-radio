Flare.

A claim board for expiring surplus, moving food sideways between Birmingham's pantry network — before the clock runs out.

RED-TEAMED

This plan was run through an adversarial counterparty pass. Verdict: build with fixes — do not pivot. Every attack and its resolution is documented in §7. The fixes are already folded into this document.

§1The one person, the one moment

Ray is the volunteer coordinator of a small church food pantry — one of the Community Food Bank of Central Alabama's ~300 partner agencies. He barcodes everything and tracks every expiration date. His problem isn't inventory; it's the gap between when food expires and when his distribution runs.

The decision moment: Monday's inventory check surfaces 120 lbs of food expiring within 72 hours. His distribution is Wednesday — and Wednesday can't absorb it. Today he spends the next hours serially phoning pantries in his personal network: "I've got X, can you take it? What can you send back so the truck isn't empty?"

Ray is modeled on a real coordinator on our team's family — who runs this exact workflow manually in western North Carolina, spends 8–10 hours a week on it, and cut his pantry's annual food waste from $3–5k to under $500 by doing it. We say that plainly: he isn't in Birmingham. Birmingham has roughly 300 structurally identical seats, operating under worse conditions — ~3M lbs cut from CFBCA's 2025 supply, ~80,000 Alabamians losing SNAP this month. The rubric's "one person" is a design discipline, not a residency requirement.

§2The friction we remove — and refuse to relocate

Birmingham's gap is not food or organizations; it's that supply and demand can't see each other in time. Grace Klein's FeedBHM already rescues donor surplus into the network. CFBCA's fleet moves food down from the warehouse. Nothing moves food sideways — pantry to pantry — when expiry outruns a distribution schedule. That lateral move is currently one man's phone tree.

Flare replaces the phone tree with one asynchronous post. The friction it removes is Ray's serial calling; the friction it must not create is notification spam for eleven other overworked coordinators. That constraint drives the whole design.

  1. Post. Ray files a flare in ~60 seconds: category, quantity, storage requirement, expiry window, pickup window, and — the field no rescue app models — his next distribution date, the reason the food can't wait.
  2. Targeted fan-out. A rule-based compatibility filter notifies only orgs that can physically take it: storage capability ≥ requirement, within radius, open hours overlapping the pickup window, opted-in categories. All matches are notified simultaneously — no ranking, no scoring.
  3. Claim & confirm. First tap holds the flare for 30 minutes. Ray gets one notification and confirms. A human says yes on both ends of every transfer — the tool never decides.
  4. Move it. The claim records who moves it: claimer picks up, poster drops off, or "needs a volunteer driver" — which links out to FeedBHM's existing driver network. Their app becomes our transport layer, not our competitor.
  5. Resolve or escalate. Both parties see the status rail. If nobody claims before the window closes, the flare escalates to CFBCA's agency-relations desk, with verified 211 and emergency links preserved on screen. A dead flare is loud, never silent.

Every flare auto-expires. Nothing in the system persists as availability data — the only claim about what's on a shelf is one a human just typed about their own shelf, and it deletes itself. Flare structurally cannot rot into a stale directory.

§7Counterparty review — attacks & resolutions

An adversarial pass ranked ten attacks. Verdict: build with fixes. The five that mattered, and what changed:

1 · "Isn't this just FeedBHM?"FIXED IN PRODUCT

The mechanic resembles Grace Klein's rescue app with the poster swapped. Resolution: the differentiation now lives in the UI, not the pitch — the flare form models the next-distribution date (the schedule-mismatch trigger no rescue app has), the flow is lateral (pantry↔pantry, not donor→network), and FeedBHM is wired in as our volunteer-driver transport layer. Competitor became complement.

2 · "Org profiles are a static directory in a trench coat — maintaining them is the relocated friction."FIXED IN PRODUCT

The killer question, and we answer it unprompted. Resolution: profiles are self-maintained by the org that benefits — stale profile = you stop receiving free food, an incentive 211 never had; a confirm-your-profile micro-prompt fires at claim time so using the system refreshes the data; and a mis-targeted flare degrades gracefully — it goes unclaimed and escalates, never strands food silently. A "profile last confirmed: 3 days ago" chip ships in the demo UI.

3 · "Ray's phone tree currently beats you — cold start kills claim boards."ADOPTION STORY

With 5 of 300 agencies enrolled, a phone call to Deacon Williams wins. Resolution: CFBCA is the anchor tenant — its fleet already visits every partner agency five days a week, its agency-relations desk is already our escalation path, and it onboards partners on relationships it already has. One institutional adopter lights up all 300 nodes.

4 · "You hand-waved transportation, and the repo told you it would check."FIXED IN PRODUCT

A match that dies at "nobody can move it" is worse than no match. Resolution: the claim now carries a who-moves-it field — claimer pickup, poster drop-off, or volunteer driver via FeedBHM. Fifteen minutes of build time; closes the repo's named criterion.

5 · "Backhaul is a second product hiding in step 4."CUT TO ONE LINE

Standing-needs matching doubles the data model and contradicts our own "smallest intervention" claim. Resolution: demoted to a single hardcoded suggestion on the confirmation screen ("Bethel has a standing need for rice — bring some?") plus one roadmap sentence. The idea's judge-appeal at 2% of its cost.

Also resolved: first-tap-claims is defensible as safety-gating, not rationing — and we deliberately do not add ranked or wave-based notification, which would make the algorithm's role bigger (REHEARSED); Ray's geography is disclosed, not fudged (REHEARSED); synthetic data uses obviously fictional names under a persistent DEMO banner (TRIPWIRE). The counterparty also weighed pivots — a SNAP navigator scores high on impact but walks into the do-not-build list; every directory-adjacent idea is prohibited by construction. Flare's problems are all answerable; the alternatives' problems are structural.

§8Demo script & the two rehearsed lines

Scenario A (happy path): as Ray — post the dairy flare, watch "4 of 12 orgs notified," switch persona to the claiming org, claim, switch back, confirm, choose transport, land on PICKED UP. Scenario B (failure path): post a flare nobody claims — show the escalation screen with CFBCA desk and verified 211 links. Say once, out loud: "every number on this screen is invented."

JUDGE: "You've turned Ray's 8 hours into notification fatigue for eleven other coordinators. How is that not relocating the friction?"

Those eleven coordinators are already fielding Ray's phone calls today — synchronously, unfiltered, mid-task. Flare makes the same signal asynchronous, filtered to food they can physically store, near them, during hours they're open — and ignorable at zero cost, because a flare nobody claims escalates on its own. The friction isn't relocated; it's shrunk and made optional at every node.

JUDGE: "First-come-first-served is an algorithm allocating food."

The filter gates physical safety — can you store frozen food, can you reach it before it spoils. It never scores need and never decides shares. All compatible orgs are notified at the same moment, and a human confirms both ends of every transfer. Rationing is when software decides who deserves food; Flare only decides who can safely receive a message.
